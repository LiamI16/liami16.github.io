Supporting code for *Systematic Comparison of Collision Risk Metrics*, currently under
review for ACC 2026. Working paper — the code is the source of truth until it's
published; this page will get a results section once the numerical experiments are in.

## The problem

Collision-risk assessment in unstructured environments — maritime, aviation, space —
has no lanes or predefined paths to fall back on, so risk has to be read directly off
the relative kinematics and geometry of nearby agents. Two families of metrics do this,
and practitioners pick between them fairly ad hoc: **CPA metrics** (DCPA/TCPA) collapse
an encounter into two scalars — how close agents get and when — evaluated at the
current candidate velocity. The **velocity obstacle (VO)** instead returns the entire
*set* of ego velocities that lead to a collision at some point in the horizon, which is
what a controller actually needs to pick an evasive action from.

Both metrics are trying to characterize the same underlying risk, but the formal
relationship between the scalar and set-based views was only partially known — prior
work showed a partial equivalence for a special case. We wanted the full relationship,
for finite and infinite horizons, and then wanted to know what survives once the
observations feeding either metric become uncertain.

## How it works

### Reducing the VO to two numbers

For circular agents with combined collision radius `R`, relative position `p_rel`, and
relative velocity `v_rel`, the squared separation distance along the relative
trajectory decomposes exactly as

```
d²(t) = ‖v_rel‖² (t − TCPA)² + DCPA²
```

— i.e. TCPA is *always* the global minimizer of separation distance, and DCPA is the
minimum value it attains there. That one identity is what makes the rest of the
comparison work: whether a trajectory ever comes within `R` over an infinite horizon
reduces to a case split purely on the sign of TCPA — if TCPA is positive, check
`DCPA ≤ R`; if TCPA is non-positive, the closest approach already happened, so check
the current separation `‖p_rel‖ ≤ R` instead. That gives a closed-form, if-and-only-if
membership test for the VO stated entirely in CPA terms — no need to sweep over
velocities or intersect circles numerically. The finite-horizon case (a bounded
look-ahead `τ`, which real systems use to avoid reacting to conflicts that are still
far off) adds a third case for when TCPA falls outside the window, checking separation
at the horizon boundary `τ` instead.

### Support functions: one implementation for every convex shape

The theoretical equivalence above is proved for discs, but the code needed to handle
arbitrary convex agent geometry (ellipses, polygons) and — critically — needed to
*inflate* those shapes to absorb positional and velocity uncertainty without
reconstructing a new collision geometry by hand for every shape/uncertainty
combination.

The trick is representing every convex body by its **support function**
`h_S(d) = max_{x∈S} d·x` instead of its boundary. Support functions turn Minkowski
sums — which is what both "combine ego and obstacle geometry" and "inflate a shape by
an uncertainty ellipse" actually are — into pointwise addition:
`h_{X⊕Y}(d) = h_X(d) + h_Y(d)`. So a circle, an ellipse, a polygon, and any Minkowski
combination of them all reduce to the same two queries — a support value and a support
point — and stacking on a confidence-ellipse inflation for uncertainty costs exactly
one more additive term, regardless of what shape it's being added to. `minkowski_utils`
implements this for `Circle`, `Ellipse`, and `Polygon`, each in closed form (`O(1)` for
circle/ellipse, `O(m)` for an `m`-vertex polygon), with `MinkowskiSum` composing them.

For a general convex obstacle, the two edges of the collision cone are the roots of the
support function evaluated along a parameterized direction `d(φ)` — exactly two roots
exist on `[0, 2π)` since the origin sits outside the (convex) collision set. The code
brackets sign changes on a grid of angles and refines each root with Brent's method;
circles get the closed-form shortcut, everything else pays the `O(n)` root-finding
cost.

### Where uncertainty enters — and where it doesn't collapse cleanly

Position error and velocity error enter the VO at different points: position
uncertainty inflates the *collision set* in relative-position space; velocity
uncertainty inflates the resulting VO in absolute velocity space. Under the
support-function representation both are just extra additive terms, so `vo_utils`'s
`uncertainty` / `uncertainty_models` modules implement confidence bodies (`Gaussian`,
`UniformBox`, `UniformEllipse`, `IndependentSum`) that plug into the same
Minkowski-sum machinery used for agent geometry — a `kσ` Gaussian confidence ellipse
has closed-form semi-axes from the covariance eigendecomposition, and its support
function is `k‖Σ^(1/2) d‖`.

This is also where the CPA/VO equivalence proven for exact observations stops being
free: DCPA and TCPA are each single scalars, so "uncertainty in DCPA" is a
one-dimensional distribution question, while the VO's boundary moves as a whole
2D set. The cover image shows this directly — nested `kσ` position-confidence
ellipses and their induced collision cones on the left, and the corresponding
velocity-space level sets on the right; the two views agree exactly at any fixed
confidence level, but a single-scalar DCPA/TCPA confidence interval can't reconstruct
that 2D shape on its own. Quantifying exactly how much information gets lost — and
whether cheap scalar bounds can substitute for the full VO under bounded uncertainty —
is the empirical half of the paper, currently being run.

## Where it stands

The theoretical side (finite/infinite-horizon equivalence, the support-function
implementation, uncertainty inflation) is done and proven; `sampling` / `scenario` /
`scales` cover 13 canonical encounter geometries (crossing, head-on, overtaking, …)
used as the numerical testbed. The empirical comparison under Gaussian and bounded
uncertainty is still being run ahead of the ACC deadline — this page will get updated
once results land.
